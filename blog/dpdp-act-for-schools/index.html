DPDP Act for Schools: Parental Consent, Children's Data and What to Ask Your ERP Vendor

16 Nov 2026 · Data privacy · ConnectEdu Team

Book a Demo School Login

A school holds more personal data about a child than almost anyone else the family deals with: admission forms, Aadhaar and APAAR numbers, marks, attendance, fees, bus stops, parent phone numbers. Most of it is handled on habit. The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 and the DPDP Rules, 2025 change that, and the date that matters is now about six months away.

This guide explains the DPDP Act for schools in plain terms: what is already in force, what starts on 13 May 2027, what "verifiable parental consent" means, the narrow exemption schools get, and the questions to put to your ERP vendor. It is written for principals, trustees and school office admins in Indian schools.

This is a general explainer, not legal advice. For decisions about your school, talk to a lawyer who works on data protection.

Where the DPDP timeline stands in November 2026

The DPDP Rules were notified in the Gazette on 13 November 2025 (G.S.R. 846(E), MeitY PDF). They come in three phases, set out in Rule 1:

Phase Date What starts
1 13 November 2025 Definitions and the Data Protection Board of India (Rules 1, 2, 17 to 21)
2 13 November 2026 Consent Manager registration (Rule 4)
3 13 May 2027 Almost everything a school has to do: notices, security safeguards, breach intimation, children's data, rights of parents and students, retention (Rules 3, 5 to 16, 22, 23)

On 14 November 2026 the government announced that the Consent Manager registration framework came into effect on 13 November 2026, and repeated that the obligations of Data Fiduciaries, the rights of Data Principals and the Board's inquiry and penalty processes are scheduled for 13 May 2027.

Two things to keep in mind:

  • Nothing changed for schools on 13 November 2026. Consent Managers are registered companies that help people manage consent; a school does not have to become or use one.
  • The 13 May 2027 date could move earlier. In January 2026 MeitY asked industry for views on shortening the 18-month window to 12 months. As of mid-November 2026 no notification doing that has been issued, but it is a good reason not to leave preparation to April.

Who is who: the school, the parent and the ERP vendor

Every obligation hangs on three terms:

  • Data Fiduciary: whoever decides why and how personal data is processed. For student and parent data, that is the school (or the trust or society that runs it).
  • Data Principal: the person the data is about. For a child (anyone under 18) this includes the parent or lawful guardian, so parents exercise the rights.
  • Data Processor: anyone who processes data on the school's behalf. Your school ERP, fee gateway, transport app or SMS vendor usually sits here.

Under Section 8 the school remains responsible even when a vendor does the processing, and may engage a processor only under a valid contract. "Our software company handles that" is not an answer the law accepts.

Children's data: what Section 9 says

Section 9 of the DPDP Act has three duties that apply to every child's data:

  1. Verifiable parental consent before processing any personal data of a child (Section 9(1)).
  2. No processing likely to cause a detrimental effect on a child's well-being (Section 9(2)).
  3. No tracking or behavioural monitoring of children, and no targeted advertising directed at them (Section 9(3)).

The second duty has no school exemption at all.

What "verifiable" consent means under Rule 10

Rule 10 of the DPDP Rules says the Data Fiduciary must take technical and organisational measures to obtain the parent's verifiable consent, and must check that the person giving it is an identifiable adult. That check can rely on:

  • reliable identity and age details the school already holds about that parent, or
  • identity and age details the parent provides voluntarily, including through a virtual token from an authorised entity, such as one made available through a DigiLocker service provider.

For a school this is less exotic than it sounds. You usually already know the parent from the admission form, identity documents and registered phone number. The gap is usually the record: a consent that exists only as a line in a paper admission form nobody can find later, or a WhatsApp "OK" from a number that may not be the parent's.

The school exemption, and how narrow it is

The DPDP Rules give educational institutions an exemption in the Fourth Schedule, Part A (read with Rule 12). It switches off Section 9(1) (parental consent) and Section 9(3) (tracking and behavioural monitoring) for an educational institution, but only where:

processing is restricted to tracking and behavioural monitoring (a) for the educational activities of such institution; or (b) in the interests of safety of children enrolled with such institution.

A separate item covers a transport provider engaged by the school, restricted to tracking children's location for their safety while travelling to and from school. Part B of the same schedule exempts some purposes for any Data Fiduciary, including processing needed to perform duties in a child's interest under law, and issuing benefits, subsidies or certificates under law or policy using public funds.

It does not say "schools are exempt from children's data rules":

  • It covers tracking and behavioural monitoring for education or safety: attendance, classroom behaviour records, CCTV for safety, bus location for safety.
  • It does not obviously cover everything else a school does with data: sharing photos on social media, giving parent lists to a coaching partner, marketing the school's own new branch, or putting student work into an outside app.
  • Section 9(2), no detrimental processing, still applies throughout.

Exactly where the line sits for routine records like fees and marks is a question for your legal adviser. The safe practical position is to collect clear, recorded parental consent at admission for what the school does with data, and to treat anything outside education and safety as needing its own consent.

The other duties that start on 13 May 2027

From 13 May 2027 a school, as Data Fiduciary, also has to handle:

  • A clear notice. Rule 3 requires a stand-alone, plain-language notice with an itemised description of the data, the specific purposes, and how to withdraw consent or complain to the Board.
  • Reasonable security safeguards. Rule 6 lists the minimum: measures such as encryption or masking, access control, logs and monitoring of who accessed data, backups for continuity, a contract with each processor requiring safeguards, and keeping logs for a year.
  • Breach intimation. Under Rule 7, on becoming aware of a breach the school must tell each affected parent without delay, in plain language, and tell the Data Protection Board without delay, with a detailed report within 72 hours.
  • A named contact. Rule 9 requires the contact details of a person who can answer questions about the processing, published on the website or app.
  • Rights requests. Parents can ask for access, correction, updating and erasure. Rule 14 requires these to be answered within a period not exceeding 90 days.
  • Erasure when the purpose is over. Section 8(7) requires erasure when consent is withdrawn or the purpose is no longer served, unless another law requires retention. Schools keep many records for long periods under board and state rules, so build a retention list rather than deleting by instinct.

Penalties

The Schedule to the Act sets maximum penalties the Data Protection Board can impose. According to the government's summary: up to ₹250 crore for failing to keep reasonable security safeguards; up to ₹200 crore each for failing to notify a breach and for breaching the obligations relating to children; and up to ₹50 crore for other violations. These are ceilings, not fixed fines.

What to ask your ERP vendor

Every vendor that touches student data should answer these in writing, at renewal or before you sign:

  • Where is our data hosted, and who at your company can access it?
  • Can we see a written data-processing agreement or privacy schedule that sets out what you do with our data?
  • Do you use our data for anything except providing the service? In particular, is it used to train any AI model?
  • If your product uses an outside AI service, what data goes to it, and for what purpose?
  • Who in our school can see a student's record, and can we limit access by role?
  • Is there an audit log showing who changed marks, fees, attendance or student details, and when?
  • Can we export a student's data when a parent asks for a copy?
  • Can we delete a student's data when a parent asks, or when our retention period ends?
  • How and how fast will you tell us about a breach, so we can meet the 72-hour report to the Board?
  • What security safeguards do you use (encryption, access control, logs, backups)?
  • What happens to our data if we leave, and in what format do we get it back?

"We are DPDP compliant" answers none of these. Compliance is the school's duty; the vendor should show how it helps.

A six-month plan for the school office

  1. December: map the data. List every place student and parent data lives: ERP, spreadsheets, WhatsApp groups, transport app, fee gateway, exam software, library system. Note who can see each one.
  2. January: cut the copies. Close spreadsheets that duplicate the ERP; move parent lists off personal phones. Our post on student data and AI tools covers the outside-app question in more detail.
  3. February: rewrite the notice and consent form. Plain language, itemised data, specific purposes, with a separate consent for anything beyond education and safety, such as photos on social media.
  4. March: collect and file consent. Use the new admission cycle for incoming students and a short drive for existing ones. File each signed form against the student's record, as many schools already do for APAAR ID consent.
  5. April: vendor questions and breach drill. Send the checklist above to every vendor. Agree who in the school decides whether something is a breach, and who sends the parent message and the Board report.
  6. May: name the contact and publish. Put the contact person's details on the website and app, and start a simple register of parent requests and replies.

Frequently asked questions

Does the DPDP Act apply to schools?

Yes. A school that decides how student and parent personal data is used is a Data Fiduciary under the Act. Most of its duties, including the rules on children's data, start on 13 May 2027 under the DPDP Rules, 2025.

Do schools need verifiable parental consent for every student?

Section 9(1) requires verifiable parental consent before processing a child's personal data. The DPDP Rules exempt educational institutions from that requirement only for tracking and behavioural monitoring for educational activities or the safety of enrolled children. For other processing, the safe course is recorded parental consent; take legal advice on exactly where the line falls for your school.

When do the DPDP Rules start applying to schools?

The Rules were notified on 13 November 2025. Consent Manager registration started on 13 November 2026. The obligations on Data Fiduciaries, including notices, security, breach intimation and children's data, are scheduled to start on 13 May 2027.

What is the penalty for breaching children's data obligations?

The DPDP Act allows the Data Protection Board to impose a penalty of up to ₹200 crore for breaching the additional obligations relating to children. Failing to keep reasonable security safeguards can attract up to ₹250 crore.

Is our ERP vendor responsible for DPDP compliance?

The school, as Data Fiduciary, stays responsible even when a vendor processes data for it, and must engage the vendor under a valid contract. Ask every vendor for a written data-processing agreement and answers on hosting, access, export, deletion and breach notice.
