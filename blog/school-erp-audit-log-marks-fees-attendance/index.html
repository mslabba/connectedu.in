Who Changed This Mark? How a School ERP Audit Log Settles Marks, Fee and Attendance Disputes

22 Oct 2026 · School operations · Muhammed Shihabudeen Labba

Book a Demo School Login

An audit log in a school ERP records every edit to marks, fees, attendance and student details, so a school can show who changed a record and when. Pair it with a simple edit policy — who may change what, until when — and a dispute routine, and half-yearly mark queries become a lookup, not an argument.

Half-yearly results go out to parents. Two days later a parent is at the office with a screenshot: the class teacher said their daughter scored 42 in Science, but the app shows 38. Who changed it? Was it a re-total, a moderation step, or a typing mistake? In most schools the principal ends up calling three teachers and the exam in-charge, and nobody is fully sure.

This is the problem an audit log solves. Below: what it is, what it should record, a simple edit policy for the office, and a five-step routine for the next dispute.

What is an audit log in a school ERP?

An audit log is a running record of changes to your school's data. The ERP shows the current value of a mark, a fee balance or an attendance entry. The audit log shows how it got there: each edit, kept alongside the record.

That is different from the ways most schools track changes today:

  • The current value only. Most spreadsheets and older systems simply overwrite. Once a 42 becomes a 38, the 42 is gone.
  • WhatsApp messages. "Sir, please change roll no. 14 to 38" sits in one teacher's phone, if it hasn't been deleted.
  • Excel files named final_v3. Several versions, no clear owner, and no way to know which one reached the parent.

An audit log answers the questions a parent, a manager or an inspector actually asks: what changed, who changed it, and when.

Why marks, fees and attendance need one

Marks: re-totals, moderation and late entries

Marks change for honest reasons. Answer scripts are re-totalled, internal assessment is added late, a teacher corrects a slip, or a department moderates a paper. None of these is wrong. What goes wrong is not being able to explain it later. When results are already in the parent app, any change needs a clear trail. (If you publish results digitally, see how results reach parents in the app.)

Fees: concessions, waivers and receipt corrections

Fee records change too: a sibling concession applied late, a waiver approved by management, a payment posted to the wrong student, a receipt reissued. Each edit moves money on paper, so the accounts head will want a record of who made it. This matters even more once receipts are used for tax claims — our guide to the fee receipt format explains why receipts need to be right first time. For the wider picture, see fee collection and receipts.

Attendance: late corrections

A student marked absent who was actually on a school trip; a late arrival recorded as absent. Corrections are normal, but attendance can affect eligibility, parent alerts and staff records, so changes made days later should be traceable. Clean attendance records start with clean entry, but corrections still happen.

Student details: names and dates of birth

Changes to a student's name, date of birth or parent details can later reach board registration. A wrong edit here costs far more to fix after it reaches the board — see our guide to name and DOB corrections.

What a good audit log should record

Whatever ERP your school uses, a good audit log should capture:

  • What changed: the record and the field (for example, Science marks for one student in the half-yearly exam).
  • Who changed it: the individual staff login, not a shared "office" account.
  • When: date and time.
  • The old and the new value: so you can see 42 became 38, not just that "something was edited".
  • Why: a short reason, ideally required for edits after results are published.
  • Whether it can be altered: a good audit log should be append-only, so entries can't be quietly edited or deleted.
  • Who can see it: viewing should be limited by role, typically the principal, exam in-charge and accounts head.

A good audit log should also be easy to export or print when a manager or inspector asks for it, and a good system should let you lock marks once results are published, so later changes need approval.

An edit policy for the school office

An audit log tells you what happened. A policy decides what should happen. Agree these points with your staff before the next exam:

  • Marks entry: subject teachers enter marks; after submission, only the exam in-charge changes them; the principal signs off on the final list.
  • A cut-off for results: no mark edits after results are published unless there is a written reason and the principal approves.
  • Fees: only the accounts head applies concessions or corrects receipts, and each correction has a reason on file.
  • Attendance: class teachers correct attendance within a set window (for example, the same week); later changes go through the office.
  • Student details: change names, dates of birth and parent details only against a document, such as a birth certificate, kept on file.
  • One login per person: no shared "office" or "exam" logins. If five people use one account, the log can only say that account changed it.
  • A pre-results check: review the marks change history before every results release, so questions come up before parents ask them.

Put the policy on one page, share it with staff, and repeat it before each exam cycle.

When a parent disputes a mark: a 5-step routine

When the next query arrives, work through these steps instead of going from teacher to teacher.

  1. Write the query down. Note the date, the student, the exam and what the parent says changed.
  2. Open the record's change history. Note each edit made to that mark, who made it and when.
  3. Compare with the source. Check the answer script, the teacher's mark sheet, the receipt or the attendance register.
  4. If an edit was wrong, fix it under the policy. Make the correction with the right approval, and tell the parent in writing.
  5. If the edit was right, explain it. Show the parent the answer script or the re-total and explain the change. Whether you share the log entries themselves is a school decision — many schools explain rather than hand over internal records.

Most queries end at step 3. The log doesn't replace the conversation with the parent; it makes sure you start it with facts.

This also protects your teachers. When a mark is questioned, a clear record shows the change was made properly, by the right person, for a stated reason, instead of leaving a teacher to defend it from memory.

Board results are different

This routine is for school records: internal marks, half-yearly and annual exams, fees, attendance and student details. Disputes about board exam results follow the procedures set by CBSE, CISCE or your state board. Point parents to the board's official process for those.

Frequently asked questions

What is an audit log in a school ERP?

It is a record of every change made to school data, kept alongside the current value. It shows what was edited and by whom, so the school can explain how a mark, fee entry or attendance record reached its present value.

How can a school find out who changed a student's marks?

Open the change history for that mark in the ERP. It only works if every staff member has their own login; shared accounts hide who actually made the edit.

Should teachers be able to edit marks after results are published?

Only under a written policy: a stated reason and principal approval. Many schools lock marks once results are out and allow changes only through the exam in-charge.

Does an audit log cover fee concessions and receipt corrections?

It should. Fee edits move money on paper, so each one needs a traceable record. ConnectEdu's audit log records edits to fees as well as marks, attendance and student details.

Can a parent see the audit log?

That is a school policy decision. Usually the school explains the change and shows the source document, such as the answer script, rather than sharing internal log entries.

Is an audit log the same as a backup?

No. A backup lets you restore data after a loss. An audit log explains how data changed over time. A school needs both.

Does an audit log help with data protection?

It helps the school show who touched a child's records, which supports good data practice. For legal obligations under India's data protection law, take advice specific to your school.
